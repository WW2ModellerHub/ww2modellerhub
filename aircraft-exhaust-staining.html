Weathering technique

How to Paint Exhaust Staining

Exhaust staining is the single most effective weathering step on a WW2 aircraft model. Done subtly, it makes the engine look like it has run.

Study the real aircraft

Exhaust stains follow the airflow, which is why photos matter. Stains on a Spitfire run straight back along the fuselage from the stubs; on a Bf 109 they spread back and down over the wing root; on radial-engined aircraft they stream back from collector outlets low on the cowling. Leaded aviation fuel often left a light grey or tan deposit close to the stubs, darkening to sooty grey further back.

Airbrush method

  1. Seal the model with a satin or gloss coat.
  2. Thin a light buff-grey or tan to around 10% paint, 90% thinner.
  3. At low pressure (10–15 psi), spray short passes back from each stub, building a soft streak.
  4. Add a darker sooty grey or brown-black core directly behind the stubs, keeping it smaller than the light stain.
  5. Add a final very thin mist to blend the edges.

Avoid pure black: it looks too stark at scale. Dark grey-brown reads as soot.

Pastel and pigment method

If you don’t have an airbrush, scrape artist’s soft pastels or use weathering pigments. Apply with a soft brush over a matt surface, dragging back with the airflow, then fix with a light matt coat. This is very controllable and easy to remove if you overdo it.

Common mistakes

  • Stains that are too dark or too wide.
  • Stains that don’t follow the airflow.
  • Identical stains on every stub — vary them slightly.
  • Forgetting gun-port staining on the wings, which uses the same technique.

Quick answers

What colour is aircraft exhaust staining?

Usually a light grey or tan near the stubs, darkening to a sooty grey-brown further back. Avoid pure black.

Can I do exhaust stains without an airbrush?

Yes, with soft pastels or weathering pigments applied with a brush.

Which way do exhaust stains go?

Back along the airflow, which varies by aircraft. Check photos.
