How to mottle a Bf 109 Gustav

WW2 Modeller Hub202510 min read

The mottled fuselage of the Bf 109 G is one of the most characterful and most feared surface finishes in scale modelling — a fluid, organic pattern of irregular colour patches that looks deceptively simple but rewards careful approach. This is a complete step-by-step guide from base coat to final finish.

Understanding the real thing

The Bf 109 G mottled scheme wasn't applied from a template. Factory paint was applied in the standard RLM 74 Graugrün / RLM 75 Grauviolett splinter pattern on the upper surfaces and RLM 76 Lichtblau on the undersurfaces. The fuselage mottling was then applied by airbrush — either at the factory or, increasingly from 1943, in the field — as a semi-random pattern of RLM 74 and 75 patches over the RLM 76 base coat on the fuselage sides.

The critical thing to understand is that no two aircraft were identical. Some had heavy, overlapping mottles; others had sparse, widely spaced patches. Some used only RLM 74; others used a mix of 74 and 75. Some aircraft had the mottles applied over a still-wet base coat, creating soft edges; others were applied over a cured coat, producing sharper definition. Your reference photograph should drive your interpretation, not a generic diagram.

What you need

  • Airbrush — a gravity feed with a 0.2–0.3mm needle is ideal. A 0.4mm will work but requires very precise pressure control.
  • Compressor with pressure regulator — essential, not optional
  • RLM 76 in your chosen brand for the fuselage base
  • RLM 74 and RLM 75 for the mottle coats
  • Thinners appropriate to your paint brand
  • Reference photograph of your specific subject aircraft — not just a generic Gustav

Stage 1 — Upper surface demarcation

Step 01
Apply RLM 76 base coat to the entire fuselage
Thin RLM 76 to approximately 30–40% paint, 60–70% thinner. Apply at 15–18 PSI in multiple thin coats to achieve smooth, even coverage. Allow to cure fully — 24 hours minimum for lacquer-based paints, 4–6 hours for acrylics. The RLM 76 must be fully cured before mottling or the subsequent coats will lift it.
Step 02
Apply upper surface RLM 74/75 demarcation
The Bf 109 G upper surface scheme uses RLM 74 and 75 in a soft, irregular pattern rather than the hard-edged splinter scheme of the Emil. Apply RLM 74 first across the upper fuselage spine and wing upper surfaces. The demarcation line on the fuselage sides should be irregular and slightly wavy — not straight. Leave the fuselage sides below the cockpit sill as RLM 76.

Stage 2 — The mottles

This is the critical stage. Take your time and work methodically — the most common mistake is applying too many mottles too quickly and producing a muddy, overcrowded surface.

Airbrush settings for mottling
Air pressure8–12 PSI
Paint:thinner ratio40:60 to 50:50
Needle openingMinimal — almost closed
Distance from surface15–25mm (1:48), 10–15mm (1:72)
MovementShort, circular/irregular strokes
Scale: 1:72 adjustmentReduce all distances by 30–40%
Step 03
First mottle pass — RLM 74
Load RLM 74, thinned 50:50. Hold the airbrush approximately 20mm from the fuselage surface and apply small, irregular patches with short circular movements. Aim for patches 3–5mm in diameter on a 1:48 model (proportionally smaller in 1:72). Space them widely — at this stage the model should look sparsely dotted, not heavily covered. Resist the urge to add more. Step back and assess. You can always add; you can't easily remove.
Step 04
Second mottle pass — RLM 75
Allow the RLM 74 to cure for at least an hour. Switch to RLM 75 and apply a second sparse layer of mottles, deliberately placing them in the spaces between the RLM 74 patches rather than over them. The goal is variation — some areas of RLM 76 base coat should remain visible between the patches. RLM 75 has a distinct violet undertone that provides visible contrast with the grey-green of RLM 74 even when both are mottled over RLM 76.
Step 05
Third pass — build density selectively
Study your reference photograph. Some areas of the fuselage were more heavily mottled than others — typically around the cockpit area and mid-fuselage, with the nose and tail sometimes lighter. Apply a third sparse pass to build density in the areas your reference shows as more heavily camouflaged. This selective variation is what makes the mottled scheme look organic rather than mechanical.
Critical technique note
The most realistic mottles are applied with the airbrush almost perpendicular to the surface, not at a shallow angle. Angled application produces elongated streaks rather than rounded patches. Keep the airbrush close to 90 degrees to the fuselage and use tiny circular wrist movements rather than straight passes.
Common mistake
Over-mottling is the single most common error. Real aircraft in period photographs often show far fewer and more widely spaced mottles than modellers typically apply. If you think you've applied enough, you probably have. Apply half as many as your instinct suggests, then step back and assess with the model at arm's length.

Stage 3 — Edge softening and integration

Step 06
Soften mottle edges
Allow all mottle coats to cure. Then reduce air pressure to 8 PSI and thin RLM 76 to a very dilute mix (20% paint, 80% thinner). Lightly mist this over the entire fuselage from about 40mm distance — just enough to knock back the hardest edges and slightly unify the mottles without obliterating them. This is an optional step but it significantly improves the result on a first attempt, producing the slightly soft-edged appearance of field-applied camouflage.

Stage 4 — Finishing for 1:48 vs 1:72

The approach differs between scales. In 1:48 the mottles should be individually distinct — you can see each patch as a separate element. In 1:72 the individual patches are much smaller and the overall effect should read as a slightly granular texture rather than distinct patches. For 1:72, increase your distance from the model, reduce air pressure further and use an even more dilute mix to produce a finer, less distinct mottled texture.

Paint brand notes

Vallejo Model Color — most forgiving

Vallejo acrylics are the most forgiving for mottling because they can be corrected while still wet. If a mottle goes wrong, you can immediately spray RLM 76 over it and start again in that area. Thin with Vallejo Airbrush Thinner or Tamiya X-20A. Work at 10–12 PSI.

Tamiya Acrylic — excellent flow

Tamiya acrylics thin beautifully with Tamiya X-20A and produce very fine, controlled mottles. They dry faster than Vallejo which reduces the correction window but speeds up the overall process. Work at 10–15 PSI.

AK Interactive and Humbrol enamel

Enamel-based paints are less commonly used for mottling but produce excellent results — slow drying time allows soft, wet-on-wet blending if desired. Thin with white spirit or dedicated enamel thinner. Work at 12–15 PSI. The correction window is much longer than acrylics — you can soften edges with a brush dampened with thinner even 30 minutes after application.
